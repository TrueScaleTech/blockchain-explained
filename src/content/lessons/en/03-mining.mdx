## A lottery played with hashes

Hashes are unpredictable (lesson 1), so there is no way to calculate which nonce will give a hash that starts with zeros. A miner changes the nonce, hashes, checks, and repeats. Each hex character is one of 16 values, so a hash starts with one zero once in 16 tries on average, with two zeros once in 256, and with five zeros once in about a million.

## Hard to do, easy to check

Finding the nonce took thousands of hashes; anyone can check it with just one. That asymmetry is the whole point. To change an old block, an attacker must redo its proof of work and the work of every block after it (lesson 2), while the honest network keeps adding new blocks on top. Lesson 6 computes the attacker’s chances.

## The difficulty adjusts

In Bitcoin the target is not a number of zeros but a 256-bit number: the block’s header hash, read as a number, must be lower than the target. Every 2016 blocks the network recalculates the target, so that blocks keep arriving about every ten minutes however much computing power joins or leaves.

## Limits

  using proof of work in September 2022 and now uses proof of stake (lesson 6).
