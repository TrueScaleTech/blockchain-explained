## A block is data plus a header

A block collects a batch of records; in Bitcoin, transactions. Its header carries, among other fields, the hash of the previous block’s header and a number called the nonce. The hash of the header is what identifies the block.

In the widget, each block’s hash covers four things written one after the other: its number, the previous hash, the nonce and the data (`number|previous hash|nonce|data`).

## The chain is made of hashes

Because each block contains the previous block’s hash, the blocks form a chain: block 3 points to block 2, which points to block 1. Change anything in block 2 and its hash changes. Block 3 now carries a different previous hash, so its own hash changes as well, and so on to the end of the chain.

<Widget />

Edit the data of block 2: that block and every block after it get the grease cross. Press “Mine” on block 2 and it becomes valid again, but block 3 is still broken, because the previous hash it contains has changed. Repairing history means redoing every block that came after.

## Valid means “the work was done”

In this toy chain a block is valid when its hash starts with `000`. The nonce is the number that was changed until that happened: this is proof of work, the subject of the next lesson. Without it, recomputing all the hashes would take a fraction of a second and the chain would protect nothing.

The idea of linking records through their hashes is older than Bitcoin: it comes from research on digital time-stamping by Haber and Stornetta in 1991, which the Bitcoin whitepaper cites.

<Note label="Simplification">
  A real Bitcoin header is 80 bytes with six fields (version, previous hash, Merkle root, time,
  difficulty target and nonce), and the transactions enter the header only through their Merkle root
  (lesson 4). Here the data is hashed directly, to keep the mechanism visible.
</Note>
